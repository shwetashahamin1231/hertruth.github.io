Iron deficiency estimate from a CBC

Logistic regression · CDC NHANES 2017 to March 2020

For non-pregnant girls and women aged 12 to 49. A high score means order a ferritin test. It is not a diagnosis.

In September 2026 the American Society of Hematology suggested a higher ferritin cutoff for diagnosing iron deficiency: 30 ng/mL or less for adults and for people who menstruate, and 50 ng/mL or less with heavy menstrual bleeding. This model learned the older WHO cutoff of below 15 ng/mL, so it will miss some people the new guideline counts as iron deficient. Use it to decide who needs a ferritin test, and read the ferritin result against current guidance.

Reading the score

The cutoff is 0.392, chosen for about 81% sensitivity.

At or above the cutoff

Iron deficiency is likely. Order a ferritin test. The model learned the WHO label, ferritin below 15 ng/mL. On the test set, 40% of flagged patients were iron deficient (PPV 40.4%).

Below the cutoff

Less likely, not ruled out. On the test set, 95% of people below the cutoff were not iron deficient. Hemoglobin below 12 alone found only 53% of cases.

When hs-CRP is high or blank

Inflammation raises ferritin, so a low score with a very high hs-CRP is not reassuring. Left blank, hs-CRP is filled with the training median (1.57 mg/L), which lowered test AUC to 0.871.

How it works

From your answers to the right blood test

HerTruth asks about your bleeding history, applies a published screening rule, and gives you a summary your doctor can act on. Nothing is sent anywhere.

  1. You answer ten questions

    How long your periods last, whether you flood or miss activities, past anemia treatment, bleeding after procedures, and signs in the women in your family.

  2. The screen is applied on your device

    Any one of four validated criteria, or a blood loss chart score above 185, marks the screen positive.

  3. You get a summary

    One of three results, the answers that led to it, the tests to ask for, and a sentence you can say at your appointment.

The four screening criteria

The questions are adapted from the Philipp screening tool, which the CDC publishes for clinicians. The screen is positive if any one of these is true:

  1. Periods lasting 7 days or more, with flooding or missed daily activities
  2. Past treatment for anemia
  3. A blood relative diagnosed with a bleeding disorder
  4. Heavy bleeding after a tooth extraction, childbirth, miscarriage or surgery

In the validation study, 71% of the 217 women (154) had at least one hemostatic abnormality on lab testing. Most were platelet function defects (120 of 217). The tool found 89% of them. It was built to miss as few as possible, so it also flags many women whose tests come back normal.

HerTruth's first question is worded in plain language. The validated tool asks about flooding or gushing, or bleeding through a pad or tampon in 2 hours or less, with most periods.

The pictorial blood loss chart (PBAC)

The optional chart turns what you used in your last period into a score. A score of 100 or more counts as heavy bleeding (Higham, 1990). A score above 185 adds to the screen, which raised sensitivity to 95% in the Philipp study.

Why it asks about mothers, sisters, aunts and grandmothers

Bleeding disorders are inherited and often never named in a family. A mother who had a transfusion after childbirth, or an aunt who had a hysterectomy young because of bleeding, can be a clue. These answers go into the doctor summary. They do not change the validated score, because the family questions have not been validated.

Limits
  • HerTruth is not a medical device and does not diagnose anything.
  • Von Willebrand factor levels can read falsely normal during pregnancy, on estrogen-containing birth control, or during illness or stress, so a doctor may repeat the tests.
  • The iron model has only been tested on NHANES data.
  • In September 2026 the American Society of Hematology suggested a higher ferritin cutoff for diagnosing iron deficiency: 30 ng/mL or less for adults and for people who menstruate, and 50 ng/mL or less with heavy menstrual bleeding. This model learned the older WHO cutoff of below 15 ng/mL, so it will miss some people the new guideline counts as iron deficient. Use it to decide who needs a ferritin test, and read the ferritin result against current guidance.
  • NHANES releases pregnancy status only for women aged 20 to 44, so pregnant girls and women outside that age range could not be removed from the training data.
  • All 15 translations are first drafts awaiting review by native-speaking clinicians. English is the reference version.

    About

    A check built by students, for the people quizzes miss

    HerTruth was founded by Nilakshya Rajora, Dev Amin and Vaibhav Appalaneni, in partnership with The Factor Foundation, a youth-led 501(c)(3) nonprofit in New Jersey. It is our entry in the 2026 Congressional App Challenge.

    Why we built it

    Many people think bleeding disorders only affect boys and men. Von Willebrand disease is more common and affects women as often as men. In women it usually shows up as something many are told is normal: a heavy period.

    Doctors already have good screening questions, but they are in medical papers, in English. We put them on a phone in plain words, in 16 languages spoken in New Jersey homes, with a summary that lists the tests to ask for.

    The clinic tool uses the blood counts school nurses already see. A model trained on CDC data points out who needs a ferritin test before anemia shows up.

    What would help most

    HerTruth is a first version. We need outside help with three things.

    1. Clinicians who speak our languages

      All 15 translations need review by a native-speaking clinician before public launch.

    2. Clinics with lab data

      The iron model has only been tested on NHANES. A clinic that can test it on its own records would tell us if it holds up.

    3. Anyone who shares it

      Send it to a school nurse, a pediatrician's office or your sister. The check only helps people who find it.
